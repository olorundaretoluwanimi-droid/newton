## The question

On Thursday, 25 October 2007, the last washers and dryers came off the line in Newton, Iowa, and 550 workers walked
out of a two-million-square-foot factory for the last time. Some left their work boots hanging on the fence. One man
carried out a sign: "Maytag, the dependability people." For a century this town of fifteen thousand had made the
machine America trusted most. How does the town that built the most dependable machine in America lose it, and what
does a town do the morning after?

## The idea that runs through it

Dependability. The washer that never broke, the repairman with nothing to do, and a workforce that counted to thirty
years and a pension. The film keeps asking what dependability was worth, and to whom, and ends on the answer the town
gave itself: it stayed.

## Three structures considered

1. **Strict chronology**, 1893 → 2007 → today. Clear, but the title gives away the end, and the century of growth
   becomes a list of model numbers.
2. **The machine as witness**: tell the story object by object: the self-feeder, the Pastime, the Multi-Motor, the
   Gyrafoam patent, the automatic, the sign carried out on the last day, the wind-blade. Vivid, but it leaves out the
   people who matter most.
3. **Cold open on the last day, then rebuild on one clock**: 25 October 2007 and the number 550; rewind to the 1907
   courthouse square (the year of the first washer); then forward, chapter by chapter, each led by a document or a
   witness, with the Sanborn fire-insurance maps (1883 → 1918) as the town's clock, until the film reaches the same day
   again and keeps going into what Newton built next.

**Chosen: 3, carried by 2.** The same grammar as the Kannapolis episode, so the series reads as one shelf: a cold open
on the decisive day, a rewind, and then the machines as chapter markers, with the workers' own words on paper cards.

## Look and sound

House rules: dark warm ground, sepia archive, paper quote cards, red only for dates and numbers. Maps used large: the
1918 Sanborn sheet with the four washing-machine makers ringed in red; the 1883 → 1918 sheets as the town's clock. Patent
drawings as evidence frames, with Snyder's signature circled. Then-and-now: the 1907 courthouse square against the
square today ("≈ same view"). Pre-1931 Maytag ads (public domain) for the Multi-Motor years. A dot matrix for the jobs.
Score in F and B♭ (piano, harp, soft strings, clarinet; a horn for the workers), ducked about 8 dB under the voice;
it thins to a single piano for the last shift and opens up again for the epilogue.

## Rules for this film

- No Ol' Lonely footage or stills (the ads are copyrighted): he lives in the narration over a real washer.
- No Galesburg pictures: every picture is Newton, or a Maytag machine/ad made in Newton.
- Quotes are only people's own sourced words, credited on the card; nothing invented.
- Whirlpool's reason and the workers' view side by side.
