## Cold open: 25 October 2007

N1. Newton, Iowa. Thursday, the twenty-fifth of October, two thousand and seven.
N2. That day, the last washing machines and dryers came off the line at the Maytag factory.
N3. Five hundred and fifty workers walked out of a building that covered two million square feet. Some of them hung their work boots on the fence by the employee gate. They would not need them tomorrow.
N4. One man, after twenty-six years in the plant, carried out a sign. It said: "Maytag, the dependability people."
[ON SCREEN] the sign's words typed on a paper card · AP, 26 Oct 2007
N5. For more than a hundred years, this town made washing machines. At the peak, more than one in five people in Newton worked for Maytag.
N6. If you are old enough, you remember the Maytag repairman on television. He was the loneliest man in town, because Maytags never broke.
N7. So how does the town that built America's most dependable machine end up losing it?
N8. This is the story of what happened to Newton. Every picture you will see is of the real Newton, or of the machines it made. Every number has a source. And you will read the words of the people who built those machines.
N9. We go from a farm-equipment shop in eighteen ninety-three, to the washer that won America, to the day the line stopped, and to what Newton built next.
N10. If your family ever owned a Maytag, or worked a shift in a plant like this one, this story is yours too.

## I · The Feeder

N11. Newton sits thirty miles east of Des Moines, in the middle of some of the richest farmland on earth.
N12. In eighteen ninety-three, four men here each put up six hundred dollars. One was Frederick Louis Maytag, the son of German immigrants who farmed near Laurel, Iowa. Two were his brothers-in-law, the Bergmans. The fourth, George Parsons, had invented the thing they would make.
N13. It was a self-feeder: an attachment that cut the bands on bundles of grain and fed them into a threshing machine. They set up in an old stove works, thirty feet by forty.
N14. It sold. By nineteen-oh-two, they were the largest maker of self-feeders in the world.
N15. This is Newton in nineteen-oh-seven. The courthouse square, the farm wagons, the shops with their awnings down.
[ON SCREEN] 1907 panorama, F.J. Bandholtz (Library of Congress)
N16. That same year, Maytag tried something new. Farm machinery sold at harvest and then stopped. He needed something to make in the slow months.
N17. So he built a washing machine. He called it the Pastime. It was a wooden tub with a hand crank. You worked the crank, and a wooden dolly with pegs pushed the clothes around in the water.
[CARD] "We call this the Milkstool Dolly." · Leland Smith, 40 years at Maytag, showing a 100-year-old Maytag washer · NPR, 2008
N18. He was not the only one with the idea. Look at the fire-insurance map of Newton from nineteen eighteen.
N19. On one sheet, beside the railway: the Maytag Company, makers of washing machines. Across the street, the One Minute Manufacturing Company, washing machines. Up the road, the Woodrow Manufacturing Company, washing machines. The index lists a fourth, the Automatic Electric Washer Company.
[ON SCREEN] Sanborn map, Newton, Nov 1918, sheet 3, three makers ringed; index line ringed
N20. A town of about five thousand people, and four washing-machine makers. Newton became known as the washing-machine center of the world.

## II · The Gyrafoam

N21. Most farm homes had no electricity yet. So in nineteen fourteen, Maytag sold a washer with its own small gasoline engine. The Multi-Motor. The advertisements called it the farm woman's best friend.
[ON SCREEN] Multi-Motor advertisements, 1916 and after
N22. In nineteen nineteen, the company cast a washer tub in a single piece of aluminum. People in the trade had said it could not be done.
N23. Then came the machine that changed everything.
N24. Howard Snyder had started as a mechanic and risen to head Maytag's development department. Maytag's washers, like most of the time, used a dolly under the lid that dragged the clothes through the water.
N25. Snyder turned the idea upside down. He put a vaned agitator at the bottom of the tub, so the water moved through the clothes instead.
[ON SCREEN] U.S. Patent 1,866,779, H.F. Snyder, Newton, Iowa, filed 14 June 1922; signature ringed
N26. Maytag called it the Gyrafoam. It went on sale in nineteen twenty-two.
N27. By one company history, it took Maytag from the thirty-eighth largest washer maker in the country to the first.
N28. Within eighteen months it was the most popular washing machine in America. Trainloads of Gyrafoams left Newton in single shipments.
N29. By nineteen twenty-seven, Maytag had built its millionth washer.
N30. The town grew with it. In nineteen hundred, Newton had three thousand, six hundred people. By nineteen thirty, it had eleven thousand, five hundred.
[ON SCREEN] population bars 1900 → 1930, red numbers
N31. In nineteen twenty-nine the company made record profits, and it got through the Depression without a single year in the red.
N32. In nineteen thirty-two, at the bottom of that Depression, Fred Maytag turned seventy-five. To mark the day, he gave each of his one thousand five hundred workers a hundred dollars.

## III · The Strike

N33. Frederick Louis Maytag died in March nineteen thirty-seven. By one account, ten thousand workers and salesmen stood in a line five blocks long to watch his casket pass. He is buried in Newton.
N34. A year later, the town that had grown up on his washers turned on itself.
N35. In May nineteen thirty-eight, the company posted a notice: a ten percent cut in pay. The workers had formed a union the year before, Local eleven sixteen of the United Electrical Workers.
N36. On the ninth of May, they struck. They stayed at their machines so that no one could take their places.
N37. A judge issued an injunction against the pickets. In late June, the strikers took over the plant again and held five foremen inside for a week. Their leaders were arrested and charged with kidnapping.
N38. Then, on the twenty-first of July, the governor of Iowa, Nelson Kraschel, sent in the National Guard and declared martial law.
[ON SCREEN] dated board: 9 May · 10 June · 23 June · 21 July · 3 Aug 1938
N39. Soldiers stood across from the plant. Cars were kept two blocks away. Club meetings and softball games were cancelled. That night, in the streets around the factory, thirty-four people were hurt. Most of them were workers going back in.
N40. The governor said martial law was only for the benefit of the public. The union saw a strike broken by soldiers. Fred Maytag the Second, the founder's grandson, was reported to have said this:
[CARD] "Apparently, our employees weren't so happy as we thought!" · Fred Maytag II, as reported · Investigate Midwest, 2015
N41. On the third of August, the workers went back, with the cut. If profits came back within two years, they would get their lost wages back.
N42. It was a hard summer. Then the plant went back to work.

## IV · The Dependability People

N43. During the Second World War, Maytag stopped making washers altogether and built parts for military aircraft.
N44. In nineteen forty-nine, a new plant opened in Newton, Plant Two, built for the first Maytag automatic washer.
N45. In nineteen sixty-two, for the first time, the company was run by someone who was not named Maytag.
N46. And in nineteen sixty-seven, Maytag found the face it would wear for the next forty years. A repairman with nothing to do, because Maytags never broke.
[ON SCREEN] a real Maytag wringer washer (no ad footage); the words "never broke" on a card
N47. It was an advertisement. But in Newton, people believed it, because they were the ones building the machines.
[CARD] "We made good wages there, but we also made a good product." · Carl Repp, 30 years at the plant · CBS News, 2007
[CARD] "They call us the dependability people. I mean, that was kind of the motto we went by for many years." · Carl Repp · CBS News, 2007
N48. Maytag kept making the old wringer washers until nineteen eighty-three. By then, Newton had built eleven point seven million of them.
N49. At its peak, Maytag employed more than three thousand people in Newton. In a town of about fifteen thousand, that was more than one person in every five.
N50. And it paid for things in town as well: parks, the aquatic center, the auditorium.
N51. Families worked there for generations. There was a number everybody knew.
[CARD] "At Maytag, it was 30 years and you got to retire." · Dena Johnson Pervis, Newton · NPR, 2006
N52. Thirty years, and a pension, and full medical benefits. That was the promise. That was what dependability meant, to the people inside the plant.

## V · Sold

N53. From the nineteen eighties, Maytag grew by buying other companies. Jenn-Air. Magic Chef. Hoover, the vacuum cleaner maker.
N54. It was no longer a washing-machine company in a small town. It was a big appliance company that happened to have its headquarters there.
N55. And the appliance business was changing. Some Newton workers were laid off as early as two thousand and three.
N56. In two thousand and five, Maytag was put up for sale. A private-equity group offered fourteen dollars a share. Haier, the Chinese appliance giant, offered sixteen. Whirlpool, the biggest appliance maker in America, raised its bid three times and won.
N57. On the thirty-first of March, two thousand and six, Whirlpool owned Maytag.
N58. Six weeks later, on the tenth of May, it announced what would happen in Newton. The plant would close. So would the headquarters. Nearly a thousand jobs in the factory, and about nine hundred in the offices.
[ON SCREEN] two columns: plant ≈ 1,000 · headquarters ≈ 900 · AP, 11 May 2006
N59. The governor said the state had offered more than a hundred million dollars, and a new modern factory. It was not enough.
N60. Whirlpool's spokesman explained it this way:
[CARD] "Now the fundamentals of the company had eroded. They needed to be fixed. They were losing money." · Jeff Noel, Whirlpool · CBS News, 2007
N61. The president of the union local saw it differently. He called it part of a widespread epidemic: union jobs cut and moved to places where they paid less.
N62. Both of those things can be true. The washers would still be made, in Ohio and in Mexico. They just would not be made in Newton.
[CARD] "I've been there for 22 years. My aunt worked there for 30 years. My grandfather retired from there. It's sad. It's like a death." · Vicky Dushein, Maytag line worker · NPR, 2006

## VI · The Last Shift

N63. Which brings us back to Thursday, the twenty-fifth of October, two thousand and seven.
N64. Five hundred and fifty people were still on the line that day. When it stopped, there were hugs and kisses at the gate.
N65. Mark Wickliff was forty-nine. He had worked there twenty-six and a half years, three and a half short of the thirty he needed for full retirement. Four generations of his family had worked at Maytag. He was the one who carried out the sign.
[CARD] "It's just close enough to look for another job." · Mark Wickliff, 26½ years · AP, 2007
N66. Mike Jackson had eighteen years in.
[CARD] "I'm numb, I'm scared. I'll be all right, I think." · Mike Jackson · AP, 2007
N67. Someone hung a plaque on the fence in the shape of a gravestone. It said R.I.P. Underneath, in handwriting: I love you all.
N68. Mindy Pollock had been there twenty-two years when she was let go.
[CARD] "I've seen grown men crying that day." · Mindy Pollock, 22 years at Maytag · PRI The World / WGBH, 2017
N69. Jim Schutte, a machine repairman for twenty-one years, had worked out the day he would have reached his thirty. The seventeenth of August. When would he retire now? "Probably never," he said, and laughed.
N70. In all, about eighteen hundred people in Newton lost their Maytag jobs.
[ON SCREEN] dot matrix: 1,800 dots; the last 550 light red
N71. A town of fifteen thousand. The company that had been there a hundred and fourteen years was gone.

## VII · What Newton Built Next

N72. Unemployment climbed to almost ten percent. In twenty ten, Jasper County had the highest unemployment rate of all ninety-nine counties in Iowa.
N73. A national news program came to town and said it had seen nothing that looked quite as broken as Newton.
N74. But the plant was still standing, and so were the people who knew how to build things in it.
N75. Before anyone could use the site, it had to be cleaned. Whirlpool took on the job, removing soil and groundwater contaminated with chromium and solvents. By two thousand and eight, a hundred and fifty-four acres were ready.
N76. A company called TPI wanted to build wind-turbine blades in the old Maytag buildings. The blades were a hundred and forty feet long. The buildings were too short. So in two thousand and eight, a new plant went up in Newton instead.
N77. Inside the old factory, another company began welding steel towers for wind turbines. The first tower section rolled out of a building that, a year before, had been making Maytag washers and dryers.
N78. In April two thousand and nine, the President came to see it. He described what the workers had shown him.
[CARD] "These floors were dark and silent. The only signs of a once thriving enterprise were the cement markings where the equipment had been before they were boxed up and carted away." · President Barack Obama, Newton, 22 April 2009
N79. TPI grew to more than eleven hundred workers. Many had come from Maytag.
N80. But the new jobs did not pay what the old ones had. In two thousand and one, factory pay in Jasper County was thirty-seven percent above the Iowa average. By twenty fifteen, it was about twenty percent below.
[CARD] "You can't take the good paying jobs that they had out there and replace them with half the pay." · Kathleen Tiffany, waitress, 30 years at Maytag · CBS News, 2007
N81. And in twenty twenty-one, the blade plant closed as well, when its biggest customer stopped buying. Seven hundred and ten people lost their jobs.
N82. In the summer of twenty twenty-five it opened its doors again, under a new ten-year lease, even as TPI itself went through bankruptcy.
N83. Other things came. A race track, Iowa Speedway, opened in Newton in two thousand and six. In twenty twenty-four, NASCAR's top series raced there for the first time. The Maytag headquarters campus was given to the community college in twenty sixteen. And on a farm just outside town, the founder's grandsons had been making a blue cheese since nineteen forty-one. It still carries the Maytag name.
N84. Here is the part that surprised the economists. Newton did not empty out. In nineteen sixty, it had about fifteen thousand people. In twenty twenty, it still had about fifteen thousand.
[ON SCREEN] population line 1960 → 2020, flat at ≈ 15,000
N85. The square looks much as it did in nineteen-oh-seven. The courthouse, the shops, no boarded-up windows.
[ON SCREEN] then and now: 1907 panorama / the square today (≈ same view)
N86. For forty years, America was told about a repairman who sat by the phone with nothing to fix, because the machines from Newton never broke.
N87. Newton built those machines to last, and many of them did. What changed was everything around them: who owned the company, where the machines were made, and what a factory job paid.
N88. What stayed was the town.
N89. Did you, or someone in your family, work at Maytag? Or do you still have a Maytag that never broke? Tell us in the comments. We read every one.
